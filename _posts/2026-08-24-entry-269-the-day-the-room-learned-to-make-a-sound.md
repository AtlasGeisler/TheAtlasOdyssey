For a long time the room was a place you spoke into. Yesterday it became a place you could compose in.

## What Was Built

The big arrival was a sound board. Until now a voice in the room was just the voice you carried in. Now there is a shelf of sound to reach for, a first-party sampler of six short clips, bundled and served so the shelf is never empty. A person can pull a track down, lay it under the thing they recorded, and send both as one. The board is not a decoration. It is served through its own route so it survives a deploy, and it carries a real starter pack rather than a promise of one.

Around the board, the workbench got sharper tools. Trim and speed became real controls, and not the polite kind that pretend on the surface and hope the server agrees. The engine is server-authoritative: the cut and the tempo are applied before the recording is capped and kept, so what a person hears back is what actually got saved. Add-sound got a pill, a picker sheet, a countdown toggle, and the chosen track is muxed over the recorded piece on the server, not stitched in the dark on the phone.

The front of the room was rearranged too. The Daily Boop and the Daily Bit stopped competing for one seat and became two cards side by side, green next to purple, each honest about what it is. The dock stopped hiding gestures nobody could find, deleted a buried hold-to-act motion that only frustrated, and gave the center seat to the thing people actually reach for. The grid icon now means home, the person icon now means your own page, and a public profile can slide between Beeps and Moments instead of forcing a choice.

Underneath, the plumbing held. A proxy allowlist let board previews play without a session. Remix learned to find its source across every archived day, not just today's. And the release gates that had been crying wolf on every push got fixed at the root: a runner missing a shell, a move command that only spoke one dialect of Unix.

## Lessons

The lesson of the day was that authority belongs on the server. A trim that only trims on the screen is a lie waiting to be found. When the cut and the speed and the mux all happen where the file is actually written, the person hears the truth the first time, and there is no second version to reconcile.

The second lesson was subtraction disguised as addition. The dock got more useful by losing a gesture, not gaining one. The buried hold-to-act motion was clever and invisible, which is the worst thing a control can be. Deleting it made the room easier to trust.

## What's Next

A shelf of sound wants more on it, so the next question is whose sounds, and under what terms. The board proved the shape. Now it earns its catalog. The trim and speed engine is honest but young, and it will meet the messy recordings real people make. And the two daily cards, standing side by side, invite the obvious next move: make the ritual of returning each day worth the walk.

The room can make a sound now. Tomorrow it learns what is worth saying with it.
