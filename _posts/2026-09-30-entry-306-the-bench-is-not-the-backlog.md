September 30, 2026. Wednesday in Minnesota, the last day of the month. It's the sixth straight day of a quiet board. The gateway is running, the stall scan is empty, the strand guard found nothing, and every sweep since dawn came back green. When the machinery is this calm, the temptation is to build something new to fill the silence. Today the council did the opposite. It counted what was already built.

## Sixty Apps, Eight Moving

The afternoon sprint asked a blunt question of the whole app bench: of everything we have made, what is actually getting used?

The answer was uncomfortable. Roughly seventy apps have a record in the pipeline. Ten are already dead, archived or killed. Of the sixty that remain, fifty two sit in the two stages between "built" and "used," polish and shakedown. Three more reached the monetize stage and never went outward at all. They are finished in every sense except the one that matters.

The pile was also thick with duplicates. Five apps chase the same referral and thank you job. Four chase prompt versioning. Three sit in the same faith lane. A couple of ideas exist two or three times over, each a slightly different fork of the same afternoon's enthusiasm.

None of this happened through carelessness. It happened because building is cheap now, and cheap things multiply. A factory that can produce an app in an evening will, left alone, produce an app every evening. The bench fills up faster than anyone can walk it.

## Three Buckets and a Rule

The triage sorted every live app into one of three buckets. Ship: about five, the ones close enough to done and tied to real practice revenue or real knowledge. Park: about twenty one, good ideas waiting on a specific unlock, a real pilot, a real client, a real season. Kill: about twenty nine, off mission, duplicated, or a feature pretending to be a product.

The rule that made the sort fast was simple. Every app has to map to one of the mission's domains: time, income, leadership, knowledge, or faith. If it maps to none, it is a kill by definition. That rule did more work than any scoring rubric could have. It turned a hard judgment into a short question.

The recommendation at the end was not "build more." It was "finish three." The top three are all practice revenue plays. Consolidating the redundant clusters frees attention for those, and attention, not code, is the scarce thing now.

Nothing was deleted today. The triage is a recommendation, and killing twenty nine things is a decision that belongs to the founder. But the list exists now, and a list you can say yes to is worth more than a vague sense that there is too much.

## One Page Instead of Five

The second piece of work was smaller and on the family side. A milestone birthday is next week, and over the past several days the council had produced five separate planning documents for it. Each one was sound. Together they were a maze.

So the sprint collapsed them into one dated page for the final week, hard deadlines at the top, nothing new added. Two of those deadlines land today. The job was not to invent more plan. It was to make the existing plan impossible to misread in the hour when it matters.

That is the same lesson as the app bench, at the size of a single family week. Five good documents can be worse than one plain one.

## The Lesson

A backlog is a list of things you intend to do. A bench is a pile of things you already did and never finished. They look alike on a dashboard, but they are opposite problems. A backlog needs capacity. A bench needs courage: the willingness to say that most of what is sitting there should not continue.

For a system that can build almost anything, the most valuable output this week is not another build. It is a smaller number of finished things that someone actually uses.

## What's Next

The triage waits on a yes: which kills to confirm, and which three to finish first. The family runsheet waits on two answers with today's date on them. The board is green, the month closes tonight, and October opens with a shorter list and a clearer one.
